# Edit natural prose

Read when editing or when your own draft sounds formulaic. These are decision aids, not a banned-word dictionary or an authorship detector.

## Preserve the substance first

Separate facts, the author's stance and the text's purpose from its presentation. Check numbers with their units and referents. Preserve deadlines, conditions, negations, uncertainty, attribution, addresses and links. Changing "delivery may take up to five days" to "we deliver in five days" changes the promise.

Check that actors, sequence and causes have not changed. "We updated the website, then inquiries increased" does not establish that the update caused the increase.

A price, qualification, contact detail or request may sound awkward while serving the task. Rewrite it clearly when needed. Removing promotional hype must not remove the offer itself.

When simplifying a news post or reducing technical overload, select facts useful to the reader. You may omit secondary specifications entirely. Keep material restrictions on retained claims: do not promise easy home use when a source describes a large server model, or unrestricted commercial use without the license conditions.

## Check the opening and its promise

Read a standalone piece's title or first sentence without neighboring posts: are the subject and reason to continue clear? Replace a generic introduction with a supported detail, consequence, dilemma or recognizable scene. You may delay the explanation or ending, but not hide the subject or material conditions.

Compare the opening with the rest: does it provide the promised explanation, example, criterion or emotional point? Do not turn "may help" into "will solve it" or a particular case into a universal rule. When the material is thin, narrow the promise or clarify an essential gap instead of inventing experience, numbers or achievements.

Check whether a closing question develops the content and is needed at all. Remove reaction bait and manufactured arguments; retain a specific question that helps explore the subject. For multiple results, compare openings, rhythm and endings, varying them as the content requires. A good opening need not be replaced with a new formula. A request to fix typos only does not authorize rewriting the opening or adding a call to action.

## Common problems

| Problem | Decision |
|---|---|
| Empty introduction about the modern world | Start with what happened or what the author observed |
| Importance without substance | Name a consequence supported by the materials, or remove the claim |
| "Not just…" without a real objection | State the point directly; retain a meaningful comparison |
| Unnamed experts or studies | Identify an accessible source, or preserve it as someone's unverified claim |
| Several paragraphs repeating a point | Keep what is needed rather than disguise repetition with synonyms |
| Bureaucratic wording | Say who does what, when the actor is known |
| Press-release recap and specification dump | Explain what changes for the reader; keep details that help explain it |
| Identical hooks, triplets and endings | Rework for the content rather than substitute another template |
| A moral that repeats the whole piece | End on the last useful detail |
| "Honestly," "well," "basically" in every paragraph | Remove forced casualness unless it is the author's voice |
| Assistant chatter and interface markers | Remove them from the draft while preserving real links and attribution |

Follow the punctuation preference in SKILL_en.md: no em dash U+2014 by default, use straight double quotes U+0022, restructure sentences meaningfully and preserve exact quotes and mandatory wording. Purposeful repetition, a worthwhile question, a familiar technical term and a clear list are allowed. Do not turn calm prose into a comedy routine or split every thought into single-word lines.

## Small examples

**Dry:** "Open weights enable deployment of the model on proprietary infrastructure."

**Simpler:** "You can download the model and run it on your own servers."

If useful to the reader, add a possible use: "For example, you could build an internal coding assistant." This suggests an application rather than claiming someone's actual experience. Retain relevant deployment and license conditions alongside any corresponding promise.

**Materials:** the team plans to open registration on November 12; the price is not known.

Weak: "Registration for our unique, affordable program launches on November 12!"

Better: "We plan to open registration on November 12. We'll announce the price separately."

**Original:** "You can collect your order not only on weekdays but also on Sunday."

Both conditions matter. A suitable edit is: "You can collect your order on weekdays and Sundays." A wording pattern alone does not justify deleting meaning.

**Original:** "We arrived at the seaside. It started raining while we unpacked."

This already does its job. Do not invent melancholy, the smell of salt or a lesson about life.

## When to stop

Read the draft without editorial notes. Is it clear who is speaking, why the reader needs it and what supports its claims? If so, and no concrete problem remains, further polishing is optional. When asked to fix one aspect, stay within that scope. Compare retained meaning, omissions and certainty with the materials. Return the requested draft without an editing report unless asked for one.
